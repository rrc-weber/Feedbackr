WBPAs have been part of UK postgraduate training since the late 1990s, with wider uptake after the 2007 PMETB guide to good practice [1]. They do two jobs at once: they guide development through feedback and reflection, and they evidence the standard of work achieved. Done properly, they reach the top of Miller's pyramid and Bloom's taxonomy: shows how, does, creates [2,3]. The ideal is a sequence. A real case the resident managed; a protected conversation soon afterwards; the educator drawing out the resident's reasoning; specific feedback on what to do next; and a record written at the time, mapped to the curriculum and signed off. Hold that picture in mind for the next slide.

Let me walk you through how this usually goes. This is illustrative, but I suspect everyone here will recognise it. 09:10 on a Monday: a CT2 resident manages an unanticipated difficult airway well. “Can we do a CBD on that?” “Yes, definitely, later.” CLICK The list runs back to back. There is no gap, and at the end the consultant goes straight to on-call. CLICK Then the rota: the resident is on nights, the consultant is on leave. The moment passes. CLICK On day 11 the resident writes it up from memory and sends the assessment request. CLICK On day 19 the consultant reads a short summary between cases, writes “Good case, well managed”, and signs. CLICK The assessment is complete and the portfolio is satisfied. But nothing records what the resident thought, what they would change, or what to do next. The evidence was recorded; the learning conversation was lost. This pattern of retrospective completion and thin, non-specific feedback is well described [4-6].

Four barriers sit between the intent and the reality of a WBA, and they compound: if the discussion does not happen, there is nothing to reflect on; if reflection is thin, the record is thin; and if the record is written later from memory, the written feedback that follows is late, generic and often never read. CLICK And it is now compounded by unguided AI use. This post is one resident's account of being accused by their ES of using AI for portfolio reflections. It is unmeasured in specialty training, and the Colleges warn it risks educational value, probity and equity. If asked for data: of 906 UK surgical trainees, about 90% were aware of inaccurate or misrepresentative WBA entries; 55.4% felt ARCP rewards quantity over quality (Beamish 2020). The Reddit post is reused from the ATRG deck: an individual, unverified account.

Responsible by design

Modelling responsible AI in medical education

Consent-gated
Educator and resident each confirm their own participation before recording starts
No patients recorded
Staff educational conversations only; patients are never participants and identifiers are excluded
Supports existing controls
The resident owns the narrative, the educator approves it through the existing portfolio process
EU data residency
LLM inference migrated to Azure OpenAI (UK South)
Zero retention
Audio deleted after transcription; neither conversation nor draft retained by the service; the draft exists only in the user's browser
Aggregate analytics only
Non-linkable usage events with no user identifier, aligned with the NHS England ambient voice technology framework
Our small group includes experience from the Topol, Clinical AI and NHS Entrepreneur fellowships. We have looked to purposefully design this tool with current and future regulation in mind. 4:15-5:30. This slide usually draws the questions. Zero retention is the headline: there is no server-side store of conversations or drafts to breach. Analytics cannot identify users or link sessions. A DPIA and a voluntary clinical-safety hazard log sit behind the study deployment. Contrast with shadow AI use pasted into consumer chatbots.

National Evaluation

Research question

Can Feedbackr be delivered and evaluated feasibly in UK anaesthetic WBAs, and what signals emerge in accessibility, acceptability, implementation and feedback quality?

Aims
  • Describe experience of WBPA process with and without Feedbackr
  • Describe usability (SUS) with and without Feedbackr
  • Explore implementation experience at focus sites
  • Explore blinded QuAL ratings of WBAs with and without Feedbackr
  • Describe assessment volumes, recording duration and failure counts
  • Provide economic modelling of Feedbackr implementation

CLICK Economic panel: early, pre-data decision-tree cost-consequence model (Snooks, Julius, Crichton). Central case saves about 24 minutes of staff time per completed WBA (16 resident, 8 educator), about £34 at PSSRU-style unit costs; pessimistic to optimistic scenarios give 2 to 40 minutes and £2 to £57. Nationally in anaesthesia (~240,000 WBAs/year) that is roughly £8.3m a year, range £0.45m to £13.7m. Caveats if asked: capacity released, not cash; the four time parameters drive the result and the national surveys will measure them; the model will be pre-registered and recalibrated.
